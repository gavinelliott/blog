---
title: 'Gav, do you still design?'
description: 'What moving away from hands-on design taught me about leadership, happiness and how I measure career progression now.'
pubDate: '2026-11-23'
---

For a long time, I measured progress by how far I’d moved away from the work.

Bigger teams. More responsibility. A more senior title.

I’m less convinced by that measure now.

Earlier in my career, I thought progression meant constant promotion.

Bigger titles, bigger companies, bigger teams…

For a long time, I chased it.

When I led a team of 60-70, I remember one of my designers asked me an important question.

“Gav, do you still design?”

I paused.

“I do still design, but now I design teams, processes and ways of working to enable you and other team members to do the best work of their lives.”

In essence, it was design, just a different kind.

And for a while, I was quite happy doing that.

Managing big teams meant constantly switching context, making sure everyone was OK, the work was progressing and the quality was high.

My responsibilities became developing career frameworks, supporting the design community and helping design, product and engineering work well together, while making sure our services delivered for users.

But I was no longer doing the practical work of designing those products and services, and it became something I missed.

As my roles grew, I moved further away from the practical work. Over time, I realised I was missing a part of the job that made me happy.

The kind of happiness you feel when you learn something, act on it and see the output of it.

I didn’t often feel that in senior management positions.

But now, with a smaller team and more time ‘on the tools’, I’m having the time of my life. I’m designing, I’m building, I’m working with my designers to help guide, critique or sometimes be taught by them, and it’s an amazing feeling.

Every day I seem to learn something new. I’d forgotten how good that feels.

I still want to lead. I still care about helping designers grow and creating the conditions for good work.

But I’ve learnt that I want to be involved in making it too.

For years, I understood what the next step in my career was supposed to look like. I’m not sure I stopped often enough to ask whether I actually wanted the day-to-day life that came with it.

I’m asking that question now.

How much of my week do I spend doing things I enjoy? What am I learning? Do I finish the day feeling satisfied with something I’ve helped make?

Those questions matter more to me than they used to.

Progression to me now is learning as much as I can, because if you ain’t learning, you ain’t living.

And of course, having fun along the way.
